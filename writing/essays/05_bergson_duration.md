# Henri Bergson — Duration and the Irreversibility of Judgment

## Overview

Henri Bergson’s philosophy centers on a single, decisive distinction: the difference between lived time (*durée*) and spatialized time. In *Time and Free Will* and later works, Bergson argues that modern thought systematically distorts cognition by treating time as a series of discrete, measurable units rather than as a continuous, qualitative flow. Judgment, on this account, is not merely affected by this distortion—it is rendered unintelligible by it.

Bergson’s contribution is to show that judgment depends on duration: an irreducible continuity in which past, present, and future interpenetrate. When this continuity is replaced with spatialized representations, judgment collapses into calculation or choice among static options.

---

## Duration Versus Spatialized Time

Bergson begins by rejecting the dominant conception of time as a line composed of identical instants. This conception, while useful for measurement, is a projection of spatial reasoning onto temporal experience. It treats moments as separable units that can be counted, reordered, or replayed.

Duration, by contrast, is lived time. It is qualitative rather than quantitative. In duration, moments do not succeed one another like beads on a string. They permeate and transform one another. The present is thick with the past, and the future is already nascent in the direction of the flow.

Judgment occurs only within duration. To judge is to act from within a continuity that cannot be decomposed without loss.

---

## Judgment Against Choice Among States

Bergson distinguishes genuine judgment from what he calls “choice among ready-made possibilities.” The latter presupposes that alternatives exist fully formed in advance and that the agent merely selects one. This model treats action as combinatorial and reversible.

In lived experience, however, judgment is creative. The situation does not present a fixed menu of options. The agent’s response emerges as the situation unfolds, shaped by accumulated experience and the direction of the moment.

Judgment is therefore not selection but formation. It cannot be reduced to an evaluation of static representations because the representations themselves are products of spatialization.

---

## Continuity and the Accumulation of the Past

In duration, the past is not stored as a collection of images. It is preserved as a pressure, a weight, a direction that shapes present perception. Each moment carries the entire past in a transformed state.

Judgment depends on this accumulation. What an agent perceives as relevant, dangerous, promising, or trivial depends on how the past has been integrated into the present. Two agents facing the same external situation may judge differently because their durations differ.

This difference cannot be neutralized by supplying more information. It arises from continuity, not content.

---

## Irreversibility and Freedom

Bergson emphasizes the irreversibility of duration. Once lived, a moment cannot be revisited except as altered by memory. This irreversibility is the ground of freedom: action is not determined by prior states in a mechanical sense, but neither is it arbitrary. It emerges from the entire history of the agent up to that point.

Judgment, in this sense, is neither predictable nor reconstructible. It is intelligible only as the expression of a continuous becoming.

Attempts to reconstruct judgment after the fact—by analyzing inputs, outputs, or decision trees—inevitably miss what mattered most: the way the agent *became* able to act as they did.

---

## Against Measurement as a Model of Mind

Bergson’s critique extends beyond psychology to any system that treats cognition as measurable states. Measurement requires spatialization. It freezes what is fluid and replaces continuity with coordinates.

While such freezing may support analysis, it destroys judgment. Judgment lives in the transitions, the inflections, and the qualitative shifts that measurement smooths away.

This is not a rejection of analysis, but a limitation on its scope. Analysis can describe what has already been spatialized. It cannot capture the formation of judgment within duration.

---

## Intuition as Access to Continuity

Bergson proposes intuition—not as instinct or mysticism, but as disciplined attention to duration—as the only way to grasp lived continuity. Intuition does not extract representations from experience. It follows experience from within.

Judgment operates in this intuitive mode. It does not step outside the flow to evaluate it. It moves with the flow, adjusting as the situation unfolds.

This makes judgment resistant to formalization without making it irrational. It is structured by continuity rather than rules.

---

## Judgment as Expression of Becoming

For Bergson, judgment is the act through which becoming expresses itself in action. It is not imposed on time; it arises from time.

Any attempt to preserve judgment by recording decisions, rules, or representations without preserving duration preserves only a shadow of judgment. The living continuity that gave rise to the act has already passed.

---

## Structural Implication

Judgment cannot survive the spatialization of time. Systems that represent cognition as a sequence of discrete states may preserve information, but they necessarily erase the continuity in which judgment is formed.

Judgment exists only where duration is preserved as duration, not converted into snapshots.
