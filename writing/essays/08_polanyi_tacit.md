# Michael Polanyi — Tacit Continuity and Personal Knowledge

## Overview

Michael Polanyi’s philosophy addresses a fundamental gap in formal accounts of cognition: the role of tacit knowing. In *Personal Knowledge*, Polanyi argues that much of what underwrites judgment cannot be made fully explicit without destroying its function. Judgment, on this account, depends on forms of knowing that are acquired through prolonged engagement and cannot be detached from the person who knows.

Polanyi’s contribution is to show that judgment is continuous, personal, and time-formed, not because it is subjective or arbitrary, but because it relies on tacit integration developed over time.

---

## The Tacit Dimension of Judgment

Polanyi famously asserts that “we can know more than we can tell.” This claim is not a rejection of articulation, but a recognition of its limits. Certain forms of knowing operate below the level of explicit representation and guide action without being fully expressible as rules.

Judgment depends on this tacit dimension. The ability to discern relevance, to sense coherence or anomaly, and to act appropriately in complex situations relies on integrations that cannot be exhaustively stated.

These integrations are formed through time. They cannot be installed instantaneously or reconstructed from explicit descriptions.

---

## Subsidiary and Focal Awareness

Polanyi distinguishes between subsidiary awareness and focal awareness. In skilled action, attention is directed toward a focal task, while subsidiary elements—sensory cues, bodily movements, contextual signals—are integrated tacitly.

Judgment operates within this structure. One does not attend to every factor explicitly. One relies on a background of subsidiary awareness shaped by prior experience.

This background is not static. It is continuously reshaped as new experiences are integrated. Judgment is thus a dynamic equilibrium within a temporally extended field of awareness.

---

## Personal Participation in Knowing

Polanyi insists that knowledge is personal, not in the sense of being private or subjective, but in the sense that it involves commitment and responsibility. To judge is to rely on one’s own formed capacities and to stand behind one’s assessment.

This personal participation is inseparable from time. One’s ability to judge depends on having invested oneself in a domain over time, allowing tacit integrations to develop.

Judgment cannot be detached from the knower without loss, because what matters is not just what is known, but how it has been integrated into the person.

---

## The Irreducibility of Skill and Expertise

Polanyi’s analysis of skill underscores the continuity of judgment. Skilled performance is not the execution of explicit instructions. It is the result of a long process of apprenticeship, practice, and correction.

Judgment in skilled domains depends on this history. The expert sees possibilities and dangers that are invisible to the novice, not because of superior information, but because of transformed perception.

This transformation cannot be achieved by transferring rules or data. It requires time.

---

## Against Formalization as Preservation

Polanyi is explicit that attempts to formalize tacit knowledge often destroy the very capacities they aim to preserve. By shifting attention from focal goals to subsidiary processes, formalization disrupts integration.

This has direct implications for judgment. Systems that attempt to capture judgment by encoding rules or representations may preserve surface behavior while undermining the tacit continuity that makes judgment possible.

Judgment resists extraction because it is integrative, not declarative.

---

## Tradition, Apprenticeship, and Temporal Transmission

Polanyi emphasizes the role of tradition in the transmission of judgment. Judgment is passed on not through explicit instruction alone, but through guided participation over time.

Apprenticeship preserves continuity by allowing novices to internalize tacit patterns through sustained engagement. Judgment emerges as a stabilized capacity only after such engagement has occurred.

This makes judgment inherently historical and communal without reducing it to conformity.

---

## Judgment as Tacit Integration Over Time

Polanyi’s central claim can be stated precisely: judgment depends on the integration of countless subsidiary elements into a coherent whole, and this integration occurs only through time.

Judgment is not a calculation. It is an achievement of continuity.

---

## Structural Implication

Judgment cannot be preserved by storing explicit knowledge alone. It requires preservation of the tacit integrations formed through sustained engagement.

Any system that captures what can be said while discarding what must be lived preserves information while erasing judgment.
