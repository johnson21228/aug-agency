# David Autor — Judgment as a Scarce, Time-Formed Resource

## Overview

David Autor’s work on labor economics reframes judgment not as a cognitive achievement to be automated, but as a scarce, time-formed human resource whose value emerges precisely where automation fails. Across his analyses of technological change, Autor consistently distinguishes between tasks that can be codified and those that depend on context, discretion, and experience. Judgment, in this framework, is not eliminated by computation; it is displaced, reweighted, or left unsupported.

Autor’s contribution completes the historical arc by showing how modern systems systematically preserve task outputs while eroding the continuity required for judgment to form and be exercised.

---

## Tasks Versus Judgment

Autor’s central analytic move is the separation of *tasks* from *jobs*. Tasks are discrete units of work that can often be specified, measured, and automated. Jobs, by contrast, are bundles of tasks that frequently require judgment to integrate and prioritize them.

Judgment appears where tasks cannot be fully specified in advance. It is required when:
- situations are novel or ambiguous,
- objectives conflict,
- tradeoffs must be weighed without clear metrics,
- or consequences unfold over time.

Automation targets tasks. Judgment remains.

---

## Codifiability and Temporal Formation

Autor emphasizes that tasks become automatable when they are codifiable—when the knowledge required to perform them can be written down as explicit instructions. Judgment resists codification because it depends on forms of understanding that are acquired through experience and refined over time.

This dependence on time is not incidental. Judgment improves through exposure to variation, error, feedback, and recalibration. It cannot be trained instantaneously or transferred without loss.

Judgment, therefore, is not merely scarce because it is complex. It is scarce because it is time-formed.

---

## Displacement Without Replacement

Autor’s analysis of technological change shows that automation often removes the scaffolding through which judgment was previously exercised. As routine tasks are automated, workers are left with responsibilities that demand higher judgment but fewer opportunities to develop it.

This creates a paradox: systems increase the demand for judgment while simultaneously undermining the conditions under which judgment is cultivated. Experience becomes fragmented. Continuity is broken. Workers are asked to decide without having traversed the paths that make discernment possible.

Judgment is not replaced; it is orphaned.

---

## Polarization and the Hollowing of the Middle

Autor’s account of job polarization further clarifies the temporal dimension of judgment. Middle-skill roles historically provided continuity: stable environments in which experience accumulated and judgment matured.

As these roles erode, workers are pushed toward either highly routinized tasks or high-level roles that presuppose judgment without providing time to form it. The middle—the space where judgment was traditionally learned—is hollowed out.

This loss is not primarily economic. It is epistemic.

---

## Complementarity Without Continuity

Autor often speaks of complementarity between humans and machines. But complementarity alone does not preserve judgment. A system may rely on human judgment at critical moments while providing no mechanism for its development or maintenance.

Judgment requires continuity of engagement. When human involvement is reduced to intermittent oversight or exception handling, the temporal structure needed for judgment degrades.

Complementarity without continuity produces brittleness.

---

## Against Outcome-Based Evaluation

Autor’s framework implicitly rejects evaluating judgment by outcomes alone. Productivity gains from automation may obscure the erosion of judgmental capacity. Short-term efficiency masks long-term fragility.

Judgment cannot be inferred from successful task execution. It must be assessed by whether agents retain the capacity to navigate uncertainty over time.

This distinction mirrors earlier philosophical claims: judgment resides in trajectory, not result.

---

## Judgment as Economic and Cognitive Capital

Autor’s work reveals judgment as a form of capital that accumulates slowly and depreciates when unused. It cannot be stockpiled independently of practice. It grows through continuity and decays through fragmentation.

This capital is invisible to systems that measure only outputs. It becomes visible only when systems fail under novel conditions.

---

## Structural Implication

Judgment is not automated away. It is undermined when systems preserve tasks but destroy the temporal continuity through which discernment is formed.

Any system that treats judgment as an on-demand resource rather than a time-extended capacity preserves efficiency while eroding agency.

Judgment must be supported over time, or it will disappear even as it is increasingly demanded.
