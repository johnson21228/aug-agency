# The Scarcity of Judgment in an Age of Abundant Intelligence

For two centuries, technological progress followed a pattern that felt almost comforting. Machines replaced specific tasks. Productivity rose. Some jobs disappeared. New ones emerged. The economy reorganized itself around the new capabilities. Dislocation was real, sometimes brutal, but it was cyclical. Eventually the system found a new equilibrium.

Artificial intelligence appears similar at first glance. It automates tasks. It increases output per worker. It reduces the cost of coordination, analysis, drafting, coding, and planning. But there is a structural difference between this wave and prior waves of automation.

Previous technologies automated narrow tasks. AI automates cognition itself.

That distinction matters.

The common framing is that AI replaces “jobs.” But jobs are bundles of tasks. What is actually automated are specific components within those bundles. The more subtle effect is on the formation of judgment.

Judgment is not a task. It is not a procedure. It is not a checklist. It is the capacity to prioritize under uncertainty and to bear responsibility for outcomes that cannot be fully predicted in advance.

Judgment is formed over time.

It accumulates through exposure to edge cases, through partial failures, through participation in decision loops where the consequences are real. It is social. It is path-dependent. It is inseparable from memory.

When automation removes routine tasks, it can also remove the scaffolding through which judgment is cultivated.

Historically, entry-level work served as apprenticeship. Junior analysts built spreadsheets. Associates drafted memos. Engineers implemented small features before architecting systems. The early years were not glamorous, but they were formative. Repetition under supervision created tacit knowledge.

If AI performs those formative tasks, the pipeline of judgment formation narrows.

This is the core economic tension of the intelligence transition: AI increases demand for high-level judgment while simultaneously eroding the developmental pathways that produce it.

The labor market responds rationally. Firms substitute AI for codifiable work. Margins improve. Output increases. Yet the long-term supply of experienced decision-makers may shrink, because the intermediate stages of skill formation have been compressed or eliminated.

This is not a moral claim. It is structural.

Human judgment derives its premium from scarcity. Scarcity arises not from IQ or raw intelligence, but from the integration of experience, responsibility, and continuity over time.

Machine intelligence is abundant and improving. It excels at pattern recognition, synthesis, and generation. It scales horizontally at marginal cost approaching zero.

Human judgment does not scale that way.

It binds actions across time. It absorbs consequences. It carries identity. It must live with downstream effects. This binding function is not an output token. It is a commitment.

In economic terms, abundant inference shifts the premium toward coordination, legitimacy, and governance. The constraint moves from computation to coherence.

Coherence is not agreement. It is the maintenance of continuity between intention, action, and consequence across time.

As inference becomes cheaper, the cost of incoherence rises.

A system flooded with generated outputs but lacking durable decision authority becomes unstable. Signals fragment. Responsibility diffuses. Short-horizon optimization overwhelms long-horizon stewardship.

The adjustment underway is not simply a repricing of labor. It is a repricing of authority.

If intelligence is no longer scarce, then the scarce input becomes the capacity to:

- Set constraints.
- Absorb accountability.
- Maintain continuity.
- Coordinate transitions between states of the system.

These are human enterprises.

The policy debate often frames the question as whether AI will create enough new jobs to replace the old ones. That may be the wrong question.

The deeper question is whether institutions can preserve and cultivate judgment under conditions where routine cognitive work is automated.

Judgment requires friction. It requires time. It requires exposure to consequences. A fully optimized environment may be efficient but brittle.

The opportunity in this transition is not to resist automation, nor to romanticize human labor. It is to design systems that keep humans inside the consequential loops.

This means preserving authorship over commitments.
It means making decision trails explicit.
It means separating generation from governance.
It means building continuity substrates that record not just outputs, but trajectories of responsibility.

Artificial intelligence can amplify human capacity. It can reduce drudgery. It can extend reach. But if it replaces the experiential pathways through which judgment is formed, the long-term supply of that scarce resource diminishes.

The premium does not disappear. It concentrates.

The economic narrative of this decade may be described as the unwind of the human intelligence premium. But it may be more accurate to say that the premium is migrating.

It is moving away from routine cognition and toward sustained accountability.

It is moving from output to ownership.

The societies and organizations that recognize this shift early will not be those that deploy the most inference, but those that structure it within coherent authority.

Abundant intelligence does not eliminate the need for human judgment. It increases its importance.

The question is whether we will design our systems accordingly.
