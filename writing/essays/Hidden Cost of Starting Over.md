The Hidden Cost of Starting Over

We live in a time that prides itself on flexibility.
We can change careers, beliefs, cities, identities. We can revise ourselves publicly and often. Few decisions are permanent. Fewer still are irreversible. This is usually described as progress—freedom replacing constraint, choice replacing inheritance, possibility replacing fate.

And yet, many people feel more tired than liberated.

Decisions don’t feel lighter. Change doesn’t feel energizing. Reinvention doesn’t feel like growth so much as maintenance. Even when nothing is “wrong,” there is a low-grade exhaustion that doesn’t seem to lift. We blame stress, technology, distraction, the news. We tell ourselves this is just the cost of living in a fast, open world.

But what if that explanation is incomplete?

What if the fatigue we feel isn’t the price of freedom—but the price of having removed something that used to quietly carry weight for us?

There is a difference between changing while carrying something forward and changing by starting over. Most of us sense this difference intuitively, but we rarely name it. We just feel the effort. We feel the way decisions keep coming back. The way conversations reset. The way beliefs we thought we had settled reappear as open questions. The way nothing quite seems to stay decided.

In earlier times, many things carried forward automatically. Roles, institutions, traditions, long apprenticeships, even shared narratives did a kind of invisible labor. They absorbed memory. They stabilized expectation. They allowed yesterday’s decisions to constrain today’s choices without having to be actively reconsidered. That labor was not always just, and it often limited people unfairly—but it did save energy.

Today, much of that carrying has disappeared. Nothing persists unless someone actively maintains it. Nothing constrains unless it is repeatedly chosen again. When everything is optional, everything must be re-decided. When nothing is settled, attention never quite rests.

This doesn’t show up as a philosophical problem. It shows up as revisiting the same questions year after year. As explaining yourself again to people who already know you. As restarting projects that never quite accumulate. As a feeling that change keeps happening, but nothing is adding up.

The problem is not that we change too much. The problem is that change no longer accumulates.

We often talk about this moment as one where constraints have been removed. But that’s not quite right. Constraints haven’t disappeared—they’ve just lost their carriers. Where rules, roles, or traditions once held continuity in place, now individuals are expected to do that work themselves. Every belief must be maintained. Every commitment must be remembered. Every revision must be justified. Every contradiction must be reconciled—or ignored.

What we’re feeling, then, is not oppression. It’s the cost of having to carry everything ourselves without support.

This reframes a lot of modern exhaustion. It suggests that the strain many people feel is not a personal failure, or a lack of discipline, or a resistance to freedom. It’s a structural load. A life in which nothing carries forward automatically demands constant effort just to remain coherent. And coherence, it turns out, is metabolically expensive.

Seen this way, the question facing us isn’t whether we should return to old constraints or invent new ones. It’s whether we know how to live when continuity itself has become a responsibility rather than an inheritance.

If nothing carries forward anymore, what would it take for a life to still add up?

That question doesn’t have an immediate answer. But asking it clarifies something important: the exhaustion many of us feel may not come from being too bound—but from having nothing that binds long enough for meaning to settle.
